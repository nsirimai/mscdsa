This chapter interprets the causal NLP and LLM-assisted results reported in
Sections~\ref{sec:impl-causal-nlp} and~\ref{sec:impl-llm-causal-gap}. Data
preparation, synthetic validation, exploratory analysis, and topic modelling are
not repeated as independent analytical sections because they do not themselves
establish the policy--sentiment causal gap. Their role was to define the evidence
boundary, preserve provenance, construct comparable analytical units, and provide
the frozen NMF topic spaces used by the causal pipelines. They therefore form the
measurement foundation for the analysis rather than separate answers to the
research question.

The term \textit{causal gap} refers here to differences in expressed causal or
conditional language and its organisation across policy and sentiment evidence.
It does not establish real-world causal effects, policy effectiveness, or policy
failure. The analysis asks whether policy and sentiment express comparable causal
claims and whether they organise related topics through similar causes, mechanisms,
requirements, risks, and outcomes.

Three complementary perspectives are considered. Semantic causal-claim coverage
measures whether extracted claims have close counterparts in the opposite corpus.
Causal-frame networks examine how causes and effects are organised across topics
and relation families. The LLM-assisted stage operates independently on the shared
sentence inventory and provides a smaller complementary interpretation layer.
Agreement across methods strengthens an interpretation, while disagreement helps
identify differences in their analytical reach.


\section{Interpretation of the Policy--Sentiment Causal Gap}
\label{sec:analysis-gap}


\subsection{Semantic Coverage}

The balanced global deficits were 0.3585 for policy-to-sentiment coverage and
0.3675 in the reverse direction. Their difference of approximately 0.0089 is too
small to support a strong directional asymmetry. The more relevant result is that
both directions retain a substantial residual deficit. Policy and sentiment
therefore share considerable semantic content, but neither corpus provides complete
counterparts for the causal claims expressed in the other.

The topic-level results locate this difference more clearly. P8, representing AI
curriculum development and school oversight, had the largest policy-topic deficit
at 0.3960. P0, representing AI-assisted learning and assessment protocols, followed
at 0.3763 and was supported by 604 claims from 29 documents. P4 reached 0.3729 but
drew on only six documents and is therefore a narrower result. P3, representing AI
competency-framework and curriculum development, had the lowest deficit at 0.3079.
Among sentiment topics, the largest deficits concerned online-learning clarity,
GenAI assessment confusion, and public perceptions of AI and youth employment.
The semantic gap is consequently concentrated around particular concerns rather
than distributed uniformly across the corpora.

Source support is important for interpreting these rankings. P8 and P0 have broad
documentary support, whereas the P4 result depends on a much smaller source base.
A relatively large deficit should therefore not be interpreted independently of
the number and diversity of claims producing it. Likewise, empty country-level
cells indicate that no eligible claims were available rather than perfect semantic
alignment. Country outputs are consequently more useful for locating evidence for
inspection than for ranking national policy performance.

At sentence level, the semantic method is best interpreted as a measure of
representational correspondence rather than an explanation of causal equivalence.
A useful example appears in the Irish evidence concerning teacher workload:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Policy: ``The paper stated that generative AI allows for a potential
decrease in workload thereby allowing more time for teachers.''}

\textit{Sentiment: ``...the use of AI would enable teachers to have more time
to focus on student learning.''}
\end{quote}

Both sentences are retained as causal claims and concern an enabling relationship
between AI, reduced teacher workload, and released time. However, neither sentence
is the recorded top semantic match of the other. The semantic pipeline instead
identifies other nearby claims with high embedding similarity. This is not an error
in the coverage measure: nearest-neighbour coverage asks whether related claims
exist in the opposite corpus, not whether a particular pair provides the most
informative causal comparison. The example therefore illustrates the boundary of
semantic similarity. It measures proximity efficiently across thousands of claims,
but does not itself explain why two pieces of evidence constitute alignment, partial
alignment, or a gap.

The synthetic semantic check supports a similarly cautious interpretation. Most
sentiment-topic deficits changed only slightly after synthetic substitution, although
S2 changed by approximately \(-0.057\), and no synthetic claims were assigned to S8.
Synthetic evidence therefore provides a sensitivity test but cannot confirm the
empirical causal structure or substitute for missing observed evidence.


\subsection{Causal-Frame Evidence}

The causal-frame network adds a different level of analysis by examining the
organisation of extracted cause--relation--effect structures rather than only the
semantic proximity of complete claims. The primary quality-weighted network produced
a Jensen--Shannon divergence of 0.2596. The source-balanced, high-quality-only, and
directly projected-span specifications produced values from 0.2425 to 0.2964.
The persistence of divergence across these alternatives indicates that the observed
structural difference is not produced by one weighting or projection choice alone.

This result remains subject to the topic-assignment mechanism. Approximately
31--35\% of cause and effect span assignments used document-topic fallback.
However, the specification restricted to reliably projected spans produced the
largest divergence, 0.2964. Fallback assignments therefore do not by themselves
explain the measured difference. Across 250 source-level resamples, mean divergence
was 0.3359, with a 2.5th--97.5th percentile range of 0.2663--0.4157. This range
reflects sensitivity to source composition rather than a conventional confidence
interval, while showing that the measured divergence persisted across the source
resamples.

The relation-family distributions clarify what this structural difference
represents. Causes/increases accounted for 36.8\% of sentiment frame weight but
24.7\% of policy weight. Policy instead assigned greater weight to enables/supports
(41.9\% versus 37.2\%), expected improvement (12.9\% versus 6.3\%), and
requirements/dependencies (9.4\% versus 6.8\%). Risk/threat shares were similar at
3.8\% and 4.1\%. These differences suggest that policy more often expresses
institutional mechanisms, support, prerequisites, and intended outcomes, whereas
sentiment places relatively greater emphasis on experienced or anticipated causes
and consequences.

The underlying evidence makes this distinction more concrete. A policy frame within
P0 states that approved generative-AI tools may be used to
\textit{``enhance teaching, learning and administration''}. Other policy evidence
describes AI as potentially improving teaching work by reducing administrative
burdens. Such statements reflect the stronger policy weight in
\texttt{expected\_to\_improve} and \texttt{enables\_or\_supports}. By contrast,
sentiment evidence includes statements that AI deployment has
\textit{``caused actual harm to people's rights''} through technological biases.
This corresponds more closely to the greater sentiment weight in
\texttt{causes\_or\_increases}. The aggregate difference is therefore also visible
in the causal roles assigned to individual pieces of evidence.

The matrix outputs further illustrate this organisation. The P1-to-P1
\texttt{causes\_or\_increases} relation had substantially greater sentiment weight
than policy weight, consistent with stronger sentiment expression of consequences
around rights and protection. Conversely, P0-to-P0
\texttt{expected\_to\_improve} was more prominent in policy, reflecting intended
educational and administrative benefits. A broader P0-to-P0
\texttt{enables\_or\_supports} difference also drew on substantially more policy
sources than sentiment sources. These cells illustrate how similar topics can occur
in both corpora while being assigned different causal roles.

The largest individual matrix differences should nevertheless be interpreted with
source support in mind. Some high-difference cells draw on relatively few sentiment
sources and are therefore better treated as targets for qualitative inspection than
as independent conclusions. Network magnitude alone does not establish the
substantive importance or representativeness of a particular relation.

At topic level, P0 had the largest combined structural gap at 0.3109, followed
closely by P2 at 0.3099 and P8 at 0.2959. P6 and P7 followed at 0.2641 and 0.2609,
whereas P3 had the smallest gap at 0.1301. The leading P0, P2, and P8 results drew
on evidence distributed across approximately 54--60 source documents, reducing the
likelihood that their prominence is attributable to one isolated document.

Country-level frame differences remain secondary. France showed its largest
differences around P2 and P8, with P2 reaching 1.6518. Ireland was led by P7 at
0.5391, followed by P0 and P5 at approximately 0.397. France and Ireland each
contained only three original sentiment sources in the frame comparison. These
values therefore identify where structural differences are most visible in the
available evidence but cannot support national rankings or claims of representative
public opinion.

Finally, the synthetic-frame network increased Jensen--Shannon divergence from
0.2596 to 0.3611, with the requirements/dependencies family changing most strongly.
The greater synthetic sensitivity of the frame network compared with semantic
coverage suggests that causal organisation responds differently to changes in the
sentiment evidence. The two pipelines are therefore capturing related but distinct
properties of the corpora.

The two causal NLP methods should not be collapsed into one score. Semantic coverage
identifies claims for which close counterparts are weaker or less available, whereas
frame divergence identifies differences in causal organisation even when semantically
related claims are present. Their agreement is therefore more informative than
directly comparing their numerical values.

P0 and P8 provide the clearest convergent evidence. Both have relatively high
semantic deficits and large structural gaps, and both draw on broad source support.
The result suggests that learning, assessment, curriculum development, and oversight
are not simply discussed at different frequencies: policy and sentiment also differ
in the causal roles and relationships through which these subjects are expressed.

P2 illustrates a different situation. Its semantic gap is less dominant, but its
frame gap is large, indicating that related content can be present while its
cause--effect organisation differs. P4 shows the converse pattern: a relatively
high semantic deficit with narrower source support and less structural prominence.
P3 consistently appears as the closest point of correspondence across both methods.

The overall causal NLP result is therefore selective rather than universal. Policy
and sentiment frequently address related AI-in-education concerns, but differences
become more visible around learning and assessment, curriculum oversight, and the
organisation of institutional mechanisms versus experienced consequences. These
results establish where the empirical gap is concentrated. They also expose an
important methodological boundary because both causal NLP pipelines depend on the
predefined cue extraction that determines which sentences enter causal analysis.
The LLM-assisted stage provides a complementary way to examine this boundary.


\section{LLM-Assisted Causal-Gap Interpretation}
\label{sec:analysis-llm-causal-gap}

The LLM-assisted stage provides a complementary interpretation layer rather than a
third primary causal measure. Sixteen recurrent findings survived verification,
recurrence, confidence, faithfulness, stability, evidence-quality, and
direct-comparability requirements. Thirteen were classified as partial alignment,
two as sentiment gaps, and one as alignment. Their role here is to illustrate where
evidence-grounded LLM reasoning adds information beyond the two causal NLP pipelines.

The clearest contribution concerns the limits of fixed cue extraction. Across the
16 retained findings, 31 unique sentences were cited as policy or sentiment
evidence, of which 18 were absent from the semantic causal-claim inventory.
Fifteen findings contained at least one cited sentence not retained by the
cue-based method. This does not establish superior LLM recall, because the findings
still require human validation, but it shows that the approaches have different
access to the shared evidence.

A representative missed relation is \texttt{clean\_sentence\_007943}:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``When teachers engage in continuous professional growth, classroom
practice evolves and improves.''}
\end{quote}

The sentence expresses a conditional relationship and contributed to a recurrent
Ireland finding, yet it was not retained by the fixed causal-cue extraction.
Similarly, the sentiment statement that AI use could
\textit{``undermine the credibility of our examination system''} contributed to a
recurrent sentiment-gap finding but was absent from the semantic claim inventory.
These cases show how less formulaic causal or conditional language can remain
outside predefined extraction rules.

The comparison also exposes a possible precision limitation. The sentence

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Quelles potentialités, quels risques et limites dans l'usage de l'IA ?''}
\end{quote}

was extracted because \textit{limites} was interpreted as a causal cue, despite the
sentence functioning as a question rather than an expressed cause--effect claim. It
nevertheless received relatively high semantic coverage, while the LLM repeatedly
rejected it as noncausal. This does not establish that the LLM judgement is ground
truth, but it shows that semantic matching cannot always correct an unsuitable
initial extraction.

The LLM stage also adds interpretation where both sentences already enter semantic
coverage. In the Irish teacher-workload example, policy states that generative AI
may reduce workload and release teacher time, while sentiment states that AI may
give teachers more time to focus on student learning. The LLM identifies their
shared enabling relationship and classifies it as partial alignment, illustrating
the difference between measuring semantic proximity and explaining the relationship
expressed by the evidence.

These observations support treating the LLM stage as complementary to the causal NLP
methods. Fixed cue-based analysis provides reproducible corpus-wide measurement,
while LLM reasoning can interpret less formulaic relationships and identify
questionable cue-based extractions. These results provide evidence of possible
recall and precision limitations in fixed extraction rather than establishing LLM
superiority. The LLM outputs remain exploratory and require human review, and an
exhaustive application would need to extend beyond the sampled evidence batches used
here.


\section{Integrated Interpretation}

Taken together, the semantic and causal-frame analyses provide the main evidence for
a selective policy--sentiment causal gap. Semantic coverage shows substantial
correspondence alongside persistent residual deficits, while the frame network shows
that related subjects can also occupy different causal roles. The gap therefore
concerns both the availability of comparable claims and the organisation of their
cause--effect structures.

The strongest conclusions occur where the two causal NLP methods converge. P0 and
P8 rank highly under both analyses and draw on comparatively broad source support.
P2 is distinguished mainly by frame organisation, whereas P4 is primarily a semantic
coverage result with narrower documentary support. P3 shows the closest
correspondence under both measures. A single combined score would obscure these
different forms of divergence.

The relation-family results further clarify this pattern. Policy gives relatively
greater weight to enabling mechanisms, institutional requirements, and intended
improvements, while sentiment gives greater weight to expressed causes and
consequences. This does not imply that policy is intrinsically positive or sentiment
negative. Rather, the corpora frequently assign different causal roles to related
AI-in-education concerns.

The LLM-assisted stage adds supporting sentence-level evidence. Some causal or
conditional relationships used in recurrent LLM findings never enter the fixed cue
inventory, while some cue-extracted claims appear questionable when their complete
linguistic meaning is considered. The LLM can also provide explicit interpretations
for evidence pairs that semantic similarity represents only numerically. Its role is
therefore complementary to, rather than a replacement for, the two principal causal
NLP analyses.

Overall, the evidence supports a policy--sentiment gap that is selective,
structured, and method-dependent. The strongest differences concern learning and
assessment, curriculum development and oversight, and the organisation of
institutional mechanisms relative to experienced or anticipated consequences.
These results concern expressed causal discourse and should not be interpreted as
evidence of real-world causal effects, policy effectiveness, or national policy
performance.


\section{Summary}

The analysis identifies substantial semantic correspondence between policy and
sentiment alongside selective differences in causal-claim coverage and causal-frame
organisation. P0 and P8 provide the strongest convergent evidence, while P3 shows
the closest alignment across the two causal NLP methods. Relation-family results
indicate greater policy emphasis on enabling mechanisms, requirements, and intended
improvement, and greater sentiment emphasis on expressed causes and consequences.

The LLM-assisted stage provides a smaller complementary interpretation layer.
Representative cases show that less formulaic causal or conditional relationships
may remain outside fixed cue extraction, while superficial cue matches can also
produce questionable causal candidates. The LLM additionally explains relationships
that nearest-neighbour similarity represents only through numerical proximity.

These findings do not establish real-world causality or LLM superiority. Instead,
they show the value of combining reproducible semantic and structural causal NLP
measurements with evidence-grounded LLM interpretation and human inspection. The
resulting analysis preserves the distinction between corpus-level causal discourse,
sentence-level interpretation, and actual causal effects.
