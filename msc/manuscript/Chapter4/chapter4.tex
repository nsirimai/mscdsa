\chapter{Analysis}
\label{chap:analysis}

This chapter interprets the causal NLP and LLM-assisted results reported in
Sections~\ref{sec:impl-causal-nlp} and~\ref{sec:impl-llm-causal-gap}. Data
preparation, synthetic validation, exploratory analysis, and topic modelling were
not repeated as independent analytical sections because they did not themselves
establish the policy--sentiment causal gap. Their role was to define the evidence
boundary, preserve provenance, construct comparable analytical units, and provide
the frozen topic spaces used by the causal pipelines. They therefore formed the
measurement foundation for the analysis rather than separate answers to the
research question.

The term \textit{causal gap} refers here to differences in expressed causal or
conditional language and its organisation across policy and sentiment evidence. It
does not establish real-world causal effects, policy effectiveness, or policy
failure. This distinction kept interpretation aligned with the study's defined
methodological and evidential scope. The analysis examined whether policy and
sentiment expressed comparable causal claims and whether they organised related
topics through similar causes, mechanisms, requirements, risks, and outcomes.

Three complementary perspectives were considered. Semantic causal-claim coverage
measured whether extracted claims had close counterparts in the opposite corpus.
Causal-frame networks examined how causes and effects were organised across topics
and relation families. The LLM-assisted stage operated independently on the shared
sentence inventory and provided a smaller complementary interpretation layer.
Together, these perspectives distinguished coverage gaps from structural causal
differences. Agreement across methods strengthened an interpretation, while
disagreement helped identify differences in their analytical reach.

\section{Interpretation of the Policy--Sentiment Causal Gap}
\label{sec:analysis-gap}

\subsection{Semantic Coverage}
\label{subsec:analysis-semantic-coverage}

As reported in Table~\ref{tab:impl-semantic-global}, the balanced global deficits
were 0.3585 for policy-to-sentiment coverage and 0.3675 in the reverse direction.
Their difference of approximately 0.0089 was too small to support a strong
directional asymmetry. The more relevant result was that both directions retained
a substantial residual deficit. Policy and sentiment therefore shared considerable
semantic content, but neither corpus provided complete counterparts for the causal
claims expressed in the other.

Table~\ref{tab:impl-semantic-native-topics} located these differences more clearly
at topic level. P8, representing AI curriculum development and school oversight,
had the largest policy-topic deficit at 0.3960. P0, representing AI-assisted
learning and assessment protocols, followed at 0.3763 and was supported by 604
claims from 29 documents. P4 reached 0.3729 but drew on only six documents and was
therefore a narrower result. P3, representing AI competency-framework and curriculum
development, had the lowest deficit at 0.3079. Among sentiment topics, the largest
deficits concerned online-learning clarity, GenAI assessment confusion, and public
perceptions of AI and youth employment. The semantic gap was consequently
concentrated around particular concerns rather than distributed uniformly across
the corpora.

Source support is important for interpreting these rankings. P8 and P0 have broad
documentary support, whereas the P4 result depends on a much smaller source base.
A relatively large deficit should therefore not be interpreted independently of
the number and diversity of claims producing it. Likewise, empty country-level
cells indicate that no eligible claims were available rather than perfect semantic
alignment. Country outputs are consequently more useful for locating evidence for
inspection than for ranking national policy performance.

At sentence level, the semantic method is best interpreted as a measure of
representational correspondence rather than an explanation of causal equivalence.
A useful example appears in the Irish evidence concerning teacher workload:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Policy: ``The paper stated that generative AI allows for a potential
decrease in workload thereby allowing more time for teachers.''}

\textit{Sentiment: ``...the use of AI would enable teachers to have more time
to focus on student learning.''}
\end{quote}

Both sentences are retained as causal claims and concern an enabling relationship
between AI, reduced teacher workload, and released time. However, neither sentence is
the recorded top semantic match of the other. The semantic pipeline instead
identifies other nearby claims with high embedding similarity. This is not an error
in the coverage measure: nearest-neighbour coverage asks whether related claims exist
in the opposite corpus, not whether a particular pair provides the most informative
causal comparison. The example therefore illustrates, at the sentence level, the
boundary of semantic similarity. It measures proximity efficiently across thousands
of claims, but does not itself explain why two pieces of evidence constitute
alignment, partial alignment, or a gap.

As reported in Subsection~\ref{subsec:impl-semantic-coverage}, the synthetic semantic
check supported a similarly cautious interpretation. Most sentiment-topic deficits
changed only slightly after synthetic substitution, although S2 changed by
approximately \(-0.057\), and no synthetic claims were assigned to S8. Synthetic
evidence therefore provided a sensitivity test but could not confirm the empirical
causal structure or substitute for missing observed evidence.

\subsection{Causal-Frame Evidence}
\label{subsec:analysis-causal-frame}

The causal-frame network added an analytical perspective by examining the
organisation of cause--relation--effect structures rather than only the
semantic proximity of claims. As reported in
Table~\ref{tab:impl-frame-robustness}, the primary quality-weighted network produced
a Jensen--Shannon divergence of 0.2596. The source-balanced, high-quality-only, and
directly projected-span specifications produced values from 0.2425 to 0.2964.
Persistence across these specifications indicated that the structural
difference was not produced by one weighting or projection choice alone.

This result remains subject to the topic-assignment mechanism. Approximately
31--35\% of cause and effect span assignments used document-topic fallback.
However, the specification restricted to reliably projected spans produced the
largest divergence, 0.2964. Fallback assignments therefore do not by themselves
explain the measured difference. Across 250 source-level resamples, mean divergence
was 0.3359, with a 2.5th--97.5th percentile range of 0.2663--0.4157. This range
reflects sensitivity to source composition rather than a conventional confidence
interval, while showing that the measured divergence persisted across the source
resamples.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:impl-frame-results}(a), the relation-family
distributions clarified what this difference represented. The \textit{causes or
increases} family accounted for 36.8\% of sentiment frame weight but 24.7\% of
policy weight. Policy assigned greater weight to the \textit{enables or supports}
family (41.9\% versus 37.2\%), the \textit{expected to improve} family
(12.9\% versus 6.3\%), and the \textit{requires or depends on} family
(9.4\% versus 6.8\%). The \textit{risks or threatens} family had similar shares
at 3.8\% and 4.1\%. These differences suggested that policy more often expressed
institutional mechanisms, support, prerequisites, and intended outcomes, whereas
sentiment placed greater emphasis on experienced or anticipated causes and
consequences.

As reflected in Figure~\ref{fig:impl-frame-results}(a), the evidence made this
distinction concrete. A policy frame within P0 stated that approved generative-AI
tools may be used to \textit{``enhance teaching, learning and administration''}.
Other policy evidence described AI as improving teaching work by reducing
administrative burdens. Such statements reflected the stronger policy weight in the
\textit{expected to improve} and \textit{enables or supports} families. By contrast,
sentiment evidence included statements that AI deployment had
\textit{``caused actual harm to people's rights''} through technological biases.
This corresponded more closely to the greater sentiment weight in the
\textit{causes or increases} family. The aggregate difference was therefore also
visible in the causal roles assigned to pieces of evidence.

As reflected in Figure~\ref{fig:impl-frame-matrices}, the matrix outputs illustrated
this organisation. The P1-to-P1 \textit{causes or increases} relation had greater
sentiment weight than policy weight, consistent with stronger sentiment expression
of consequences around rights and protection. Conversely, the P0-to-P0
\textit{expected to improve} relation was more prominent in policy, reflecting
intended educational and administrative benefits. A P0-to-P0
\textit{enables or supports} difference also drew on more policy sources than
sentiment sources. These cells illustrated how similar topics could occur in both
corpora while being assigned different causal roles.

The largest individual matrix differences should nevertheless be interpreted with
source support in mind. Some high-difference cells draw on relatively few sentiment
sources and are therefore better treated as targets for qualitative inspection than
as independent conclusions. Network magnitude alone does not establish the
substantive importance or representativeness of a particular relation. This caution
was especially important for sparsely supported sentiment cells. Such cells were
interpreted as evidence prompts, not conclusions.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:impl-frame-results}(b), and using the policy-topic labels
reported in Table~\ref{tab:impl-semantic-native-topics}, P0 (\textit{AI-Assisted
Learning Assessment Protocols}) had the largest combined structural gap at 0.3109,
followed closely by P2 (\textit{AI Usage and Pedagogy in School Governance}) at
0.3099 and P8 (\textit{AI Curriculum Development and School Oversight}) at 0.2959. P6
and P7 followed at 0.2641 and 0.2609, whereas P3 had the smallest gap at 0.1301. The
leading P0, P2, and P8 results drew on evidence distributed across approximately
54--60 source documents, indicating that these patterns were not confined to isolated
documents.

The country-level comparison revealed different areas of policy--sentiment structural
divergence across topic areas. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:impl-frame-results}(c),
France had its largest difference around P2 (\textit{AI Usage and Pedagogy in School
Governance}), reaching 1.6518, followed by P8 (\textit{AI Curriculum Development and
School Oversight}). French policy and sentiment evidence therefore differed most in
how causal relations were organised around school governance, pedagogy, curriculum,
and oversight. Ireland was led by P7 (\textit{AI Educator Guidance and Professional
Development}) at 0.5391, followed by P0 (\textit{AI-Assisted Learning Assessment
Protocols}) and P5 (\textit{Digital Learning Frameworks and Assessment Policies}) at
approximately 0.397. The Irish differences were consequently concentrated more around
educator guidance, professional development, learning, and assessment. France and
Ireland each contained only three original sentiment sources, so these contrasts
identify where differences were most visible in the available evidence rather than
supporting national rankings or representative claims.

Finally, Table~\ref{tab:impl-frame-gap-summary} shows that the synthetic-frame
network increased Jensen--Shannon divergence from 0.2596 to 0.3611, while the
\textit{requires or depends on} family showed the largest relation-gap change.
Compared with the smaller changes observed in semantic coverage, this sensitivity
check indicated that the frame network responded differently to synthetic sentiment
substitution. The two pipelines therefore reflected different aspects of the corpus
comparison under this test.

Overall, causal-frame evidence revealed persistent differences between policy and
sentiment. Policy placed greater emphasis on support, requirements, and intended
outcomes. Sentiment more often foregrounded experienced consequences, harms, and
causal concerns. France and Ireland showed distinct topic concentrations, with limited evidence due to only three sentiment sources each.
 Thus, findings identify structural patterns without establishing
representative national conclusions.

\subsection{Cross-Method Interpretation}


The two causal NLP methods should not be collapsed into one score. Semantic coverage
identifies claims for which close counterparts are weaker or less available, whereas
frame divergence identifies differences in causal organisation even when semantically
related claims are present. Their agreement is therefore more informative than
directly comparing their numerical values.

P0 (\textit{AI-Assisted Learning Assessment Protocols}) and P8 (\textit{AI Curriculum
Development and School Oversight}) provide the clearest convergent evidence. In both
topics, policy claims had relatively weak semantic counterparts in sentiment, while
policy and sentiment also organised their causal relationships differently. With
broad source support, this suggests that learning, assessment, curriculum
development, and oversight differ not only in what policy and sentiment express, but
also in how causes, requirements, support mechanisms, and consequences are connected.
This convergence points to policy--sentiment misalignment in implementation.
The differences concern how educational priorities become operationalised causally.

P2 (\textit{AI Usage and Pedagogy in School Governance}) illustrates a different
pattern. Policy claims had closer semantic counterparts in sentiment than for P0 or
P8, yet policy and sentiment differed strongly in their cause--effect organisation.
Related concerns were therefore present in both corpora but framed through different
causal relationships. P4 (\textit{Cognitive Offloading in AI-Assisted Education})
showed weaker semantic correspondence with sentiment, but this result had narrower
source support and less structural prominence. By contrast, P3 (\textit{AI Competency
Framework and Curriculum Development}) showed the closest policy--sentiment
correspondence across both semantic content and causal organisation.

Taken together, the causal NLP findings point to concentrated rather than pervasive
divergence. Policy and sentiment often engaged with the same AI-in-education
concerns, yet their correspondence weakened around learning and assessment,
curriculum oversight, and the framing of institutional mechanisms against experienced
consequences. The empirical difference was therefore thematic and structural, not
corpus-wide. This pattern also marked a methodological limit: both causal NLP
pipelines inherited the predefined cue inventory governing sentence inclusion. The
LLM-assisted stage consequently examined whether source-grounded relationships
emerged beyond that rule-based boundary.


\section{LLM-Assisted Causal-Gap Interpretation}
\label{sec:analysis-llm-causal-gap}

The LLM-assisted stage provides a complementary interpretation layer rather than a
third primary causal measure. Sixteen recurrent findings survived verification,
recurrence, confidence, faithfulness, stability, evidence-quality, and
direct-comparability requirements. Thirteen were classified as partial alignment,
two as sentiment gaps, and one as alignment. Their role here is to illustrate where
evidence-grounded LLM reasoning adds information beyond the two causal NLP pipelines.

The contribution concerns the limits of fixed cue extraction. Across the
16 retained findings, 31 sentences were cited as policy or sentiment
evidence, of which 18 were absent from the semantic causal-claim inventory.
Fifteen findings contained at least one cited sentence not retained by the
cue-based method. This does not establish superior LLM recall, because the findings
still require human validation, but it shows that the approaches have different
access to the evidence.

A representative missed relation is 007943, shown in
Table~\ref{tab:analysis-llm-diagnostic-evidence}:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``When teachers engage in continuous professional growth, classroom practice
evolves and improves.''}
\end{quote}

The sentence expresses a conditional relationship and contributed to an
Ireland finding, yet it was not retained by the causal-cue extraction.
Table~\ref{tab:analysis-llm-cue-diagnostics} shows that sentiment evidence
017246, stating that AI use could \textit{``undermine the credibility of
our examination system''}, contributed to a sentiment-gap finding but was
absent from the semantic claim inventory. These cases show how less formulaic causal
or conditional language can remain outside predefined extraction rules.

The comparison also exposes a possible precision limitation. The sentence 011227
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Quelles potentialités, quels risques et limites dans l'usage de l'IA ?''}
\end{quote}
was extracted because \textit{limites} was interpreted as a causal cue, despite the
sentence functioning as a question rather than an expressed cause--effect claim. It
nevertheless received relatively high semantic coverage, while the LLM repeatedly
rejected it as noncausal. This does not establish that the LLM judgement is ground
truth, but it shows that semantic matching cannot always correct an unsuitable
initial extraction.

The LLM stage also adds interpretation where both sentences already enter semantic
coverage. In the Irish teacher-workload example, policy states that generative AI
may reduce workload and release teacher time, while sentiment states that AI may
give teachers more time to focus on student learning. The LLM identifies their
shared enabling relationship and classifies it as partial alignment, illustrating
the difference between measuring semantic proximity and explaining the relationship
expressed by the evidence.

These observations position the LLM stage as an interpretive complement to causal NLP
rather than a replacement. Fixed cue-based analysis offers reproducible corpus-wide
measurement, whereas LLM reasoning can surface less formulaic relationships and
challenge questionable cue-based extractions. The comparison therefore reveals
possible recall and precision boundaries in fixed extraction without demonstrating
LLM superiority. Its outputs remain exploratory, require human review, and would need
broader coverage before supporting exhaustive application beyond the sampled evidence
batches used here.


\section{Integrated Interpretation}

Taken together, semantic coverage and causal-frame analysis support a selective
policy--sentiment gap. Semantic coverage showed correspondence alongside persistent
deficits in both comparison directions. The frame network showed comparable subjects
occupying different causal roles across corpora. Together, both methods identified
thematic correspondence alongside structural differences in framing. The strongest
conclusions emerged where semantic and structural evidence converged across sources.
Learning, assessment, curriculum oversight, and governance showed particularly
persistent differences overall. Other concerns differed mainly through either
semantic correspondence or causal organisation. Some areas instead showed closer
correspondence across both analytical perspectives overall. A combined score would
therefore conceal distinct forms of policy--sentiment divergence. Relation-family
results further revealed contrasting causal emphases between policy and sentiment.
Policy stressed enabling mechanisms and requirements; sentiment foregrounded causes
and consequences.

The LLM-assisted stage added sentence-level evidence and interpretation to these
findings. Some meaningful relationships remained outside the fixed causal-cue
inventory entirely. Conversely, some cue-extracted claims appeared questionable under
fuller linguistic interpretation. The LLM also explained evidence relationships
represented only numerically by similarity. Its role therefore complemented, rather
than replaced, the causal NLP analyses. Overall, the evidence supported a selective,
structured, method-dependent policy--sentiment causal gap. Strongest differences
concerned educational priorities and their associated institutional mechanisms. They
also reflected experienced or anticipated consequences expressed within sentiment
evidence. These findings described causal discourse rather than demonstrating
real-world causal effects. They did not establish policy effectiveness, failure, or
national performance differences.


\section*{Summary}

The analysis identified substantial semantic correspondence between policy and
sentiment alongside selective differences in causal-claim coverage and causal-frame
organisation. The strongest convergent evidence concerned learning, assessment,
curriculum development, and oversight, while other areas showed closer correspondence
across the two causal NLP methods. Relation-family results indicated greater policy
emphasis on enabling mechanisms, requirements, and intended improvement, and greater
sentiment emphasis on expressed causes and consequences.

The LLM-assisted stage provided a complementary interpretation layer. Representative
cases showed that less formulaic causal or conditional relationships could remain
outside fixed cue extraction, while superficial cue matches could also produce
questionable causal candidates. The LLM explained relationships that
nearest-neighbour similarity represented only through numerical proximity.

These findings did not establish real-world causality or systematic LLM superiority.
Instead, they demonstrated the value of combining reproducible semantic and
structural causal NLP measurements with evidence-grounded LLM interpretation, while
retaining the need for human review. The resulting analysis preserved the distinction
between corpus-level causal discourse, sentence-level interpretation, and actual
causal effects.
